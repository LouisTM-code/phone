The Lighthouse Keeper

Martha had lived in the lighthouse for eleven years when the letter arrived. It came on a grey Tuesday morning, carried up the hill by a boy who was clearly afraid of the wind. He handed her the envelope, said nothing, and ran back down the path before she could offer him a cup of tea.

She did not open it at once. She put it on the kitchen table, next to the old radio and the jar of sugar, and went upstairs to clean the great lamp. Cleaning the lamp was the first thing she did every day. It calmed her. The glass was cold and smooth, and when the sun was shining it threw small rainbows across the walls.

That evening the storm began. At first it was only a heavy rain, but by nine o'clock the waves were breaking over the rocks below, and the whole tower seemed to shake. Martha climbed the stairs again and again, checking the light, listening to the sea. Somewhere out there, she knew, a fishing boat was trying to reach the harbour.

At midnight she finally sat down and opened the letter. It was from her brother, whom she had not seen since their father's funeral. "I am coming home," he had written. "I have been wrong about many things. I would like to talk, if you are willing to listen."

She read it three times. Then she folded it carefully and put it in her pocket. Outside, the wind was getting weaker. Through the window she could see the small lights of the fishing boat, moving slowly but safely towards the shore.

In the morning the sky was clear and bright. Martha walked down to the village for the first time in weeks. She bought fresh bread, two oranges and a new notebook. When the woman in the shop asked why she was smiling, Martha simply said, "I am expecting a visitor."

She spent the next few days preparing the spare room. She washed the curtains, mended a broken chair and found an old photograph of the two of them as children, standing on the beach with their father. They were laughing at something she could no longer remember.

He arrived on Sunday, carrying a small suitcase and looking older than she had imagined. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then he looked up at the tower and said, "You kept the light burning all these years." It was not a question.

"Somebody had to," she answered, and opened the door wider to let him in.
