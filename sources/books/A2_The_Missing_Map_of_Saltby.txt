The Missing Map of Saltby

Chapter 1. A Quiet Town

Saltby is a small town on the south coast of England. It has one main street, two pubs, a fish and chip shop and a white lighthouse. In summer the beach is full of families. In winter the town is very quiet, and the wind is cold.

Ellen Marsh moved to Saltby three years ago. Before that, she was a police detective in Bristol for twenty-five years. Now she is fifty-four, and she has a small bookshop near the harbour. The shop is called "The Last Page".

Ellen likes her new life. She drinks coffee in the morning, she sells books and she walks by the sea in the evening. Nobody calls her at three o'clock at night. Nobody tells her about crimes.

"Do you miss your old job?" people sometimes ask.

"No," Ellen always says. "Not at all."

That is not completely true. Sometimes she misses it a little. Sometimes, when she reads a crime novel in the evening, she guesses the killer on page twenty. Then she feels a little bored for the rest of the book.

Her shop is small and old. It has a red door, a big window and a lot of shelves. The floor is not flat, and the books are not always in the right place. Her customers are mostly tourists in summer and old fishermen in winter. The fishermen do not buy many books. They come in for a chat, because Ellen always has hot coffee.

The summer was over now. The last tourists went home a week ago. The cafes on the beach were closing for the winter, and the town was going to sleep.

On a Monday morning in September, the door of the shop opened. A tall young man came in. He was wearing a police uniform, and he looked worried. His name was Sam Patel, and he was the only police officer in Saltby.

"Good morning, Sam," said Ellen. "Do you want a book? We have a new crime novel. It's very good."

"No, thank you, Mrs Marsh," said Sam. "I think I have a real crime. And I need help."

Ellen put down her coffee. "What happened?"

"You know the town museum? Next to the church?"

"Of course. It's very small. It has old boats, old photos and a lot of fishing nets."

"And the map," said Sam. "The old map of Saltby. It's from 1650. It's the most valuable thing in the museum."

"Yes, I know it. It hangs in the main room."

"Not now," said Sam. "Someone stole it last night."

Ellen was quiet for a moment. Then she took her coat.

"Come on," she said. "Show me."

She turned the sign on the door. Now it said "Closed".

Chapter 2. The Empty Frame

The Saltby Museum was an old stone house. It had three small rooms and a garden with a view of the sea. A woman was waiting at the door. She was about sixty, with short grey hair and expensive glasses.

"This is Mrs Hale," said Sam. "She is the director of the museum."

"Margaret Hale," said the woman. She shook Ellen's hand. "I know you. You have the bookshop. Why is she here, Sam?"

"Mrs Marsh was a detective in Bristol," said Sam. "She is going to help me."

Mrs Hale did not look happy. "I see. Well, come in."

They went into the main room. On the wall there was a big wooden frame. It was empty. Under it, on the floor, there was some broken glass.

"I came in at eight o'clock this morning," said Mrs Hale. "And I saw this. I called Sam immediately."

Ellen looked at the frame carefully. Then she looked at the floor. Then she looked at the window.

"Was the window open?" she asked.

"No," said Mrs Hale. "All the windows were closed. And the front door was locked."

"Who has keys to the museum?"

"Only two people. Me and Tom Bell. Tom works here as a guard. He locks the door every evening at six."

"Was there an alarm?"

Mrs Hale's face went red. "The alarm is broken. It stopped working two weeks ago. I asked the town council for money to repair it. They said, 'Next month.'"

"Who knew about the alarm?" asked Ellen.

"Well... everybody, I think. People talk in a small town."

Ellen walked around the room slowly. Near the empty frame, on the floor, she saw something. She knelt down. It was sand. A little line of wet, yellow sand.

"Sam," she said. "Look at this."

Sam came and looked. "Sand? But this is a seaside town. There's sand everywhere."

"Not inside the museum," said Ellen. "Mrs Hale, does somebody clean the floor every evening?"

"Yes. Tom cleans it before he goes home."

"So this sand came here after six o'clock yesterday," said Ellen. "And it's still a little wet. Somebody walked from the beach to this room last night."

Mrs Hale looked at the sand. "That's very interesting," she said. But she did not sound interested. She sounded nervous.

"How much is the map worth?" asked Ellen.

"About eighty thousand pounds," said Mrs Hale quickly. "Maybe more."

Ellen wrote this in a small notebook. It was an old habit. She always carried a notebook.

Chapter 3. Four People

That afternoon, Ellen and Sam sat in the bookshop with two cups of tea. Ellen opened her notebook.

"Let's think," she said. "Who was near the museum last night? Who knew about the alarm? Who wanted the map?"

Sam had a list too. "First, Tom Bell, the guard. He's sixty-two. He has keys. He locked the door at six o'clock and went home."

"Did anybody see him at home?"

"His wife. She says he watched television all evening. Then he went to bed at ten."

"Wives often say that," said Ellen. "Who's next?"

"Lily Carter. She's an artist, twenty-five years old. She paints pictures of the sea and sells them to tourists. She was in the museum yesterday afternoon. She was drawing the old map for two hours."

"Drawing the map? Why?"

"She says she wanted to use it in a painting. Mrs Hale gave her permission."

Ellen wrote "Lily - drawing the map" in her notebook and drew a line under it.

"Third," said Sam, "a man called Richard Grant. He's from London. He's staying at the Harbour Hotel. He collects old maps. Last week, he came to the museum and offered to buy the Saltby map. Mrs Hale said no."

"How much did he offer?"

"I don't know. Mrs Hale didn't tell me."

"And the fourth person?"

Sam looked uncomfortable. "Well... Mrs Hale herself. She has a key. But she's the director. Why would she steal from her own museum?"

"People do strange things for strange reasons," said Ellen. "I learned that in Bristol."

The next morning, Ellen went to see Tom Bell. He lived in a small house near the church. He was a big man with a kind face and very red hands.

"I didn't take that map," he said immediately. "I love that museum. I worked there for twelve years."

"I believe you," said Ellen. "But I need to ask some questions. Did you lock the door at six?"

"Yes. Like every day."

"Did you clean the floor in the main room?"

"Yes. I always clean it. Mrs Hale is very strict about the floor."

"Was anything strange yesterday?"

Tom thought for a long time. "Well, one thing. Mrs Hale stayed late. She usually goes home at five. Yesterday, she was still in her office at six. She said, 'Go home, Tom. I'm going to lock the door.' So I just went home."

"So you didn't lock the door yesterday?"

Tom's face changed. "No," he said slowly. "No, I didn't. I forgot that. I'm sorry. Is it important?"

"Maybe," said Ellen.

Chapter 4. Sand on the Floor

After lunch, Ellen walked to the beach. Lily Carter was sitting on the sand with a big drawing book. She was painting the lighthouse. She had blue paint on her fingers and on her nose.

"Hello," said Ellen. "Are you Lily?"

"Yes. Who are you?"

"Ellen. I have the bookshop. I'm helping Sam with the museum."

Lily put down her brush. "I know why you're here. Because I was drawing the map. Everybody in town thinks I took it."

"Did you?"

"No!" Lily laughed, but she was angry. "I'm an artist, not a thief. I wanted to put the old map in a new painting. Old Saltby and new Saltby, together. Do you want to see?"

She opened her book. There was a beautiful drawing of the old map. Every street, every boat, every little house.

"This is very good," said Ellen. "You see everything."

"I spent two hours on it. And I noticed something strange."

"What?"

Lily looked around. Then she spoke quietly. "The map was wrong."

"Wrong? What do you mean?"

"Old maps are drawn with ink. Old ink is brown, not black. It changes color after many years. But the ink on the Saltby map was very black. And the paper was too white. I'm not an expert. But I studied art for four years. I think the map was a copy."

Ellen looked at her for a long time. "Did you tell anybody?"

"Yes. I told Mrs Hale. Yesterday, at about four o'clock."

"What did she say?"

"She laughed. She said, 'Don't be silly, dear.' But her hands were shaking."

Ellen closed her notebook. "Lily, where were you last night?"

"Here. On the beach. I was painting the sunset, then the stars. I stayed until about midnight."

"Did you see anybody?"

Lily thought. "Yes, actually. At about eleven o'clock, somebody walked along the beach. From the town to the lighthouse. Then, later, back again. A woman, I think. She had a torch. She was walking very fast."

"Did you see her face?"

"No. It was too dark. But she had a long coat. A green one."

Ellen thanked her. But she did not walk back to town. She walked the other way, along the beach, to the lighthouse.

It took her twenty minutes. The lighthouse stood on a small hill of rocks at the end of the beach. Nobody lived there now. The light did not work. The door was old and heavy, and it was locked.

Ellen looked at the ground in front of the door. The sand there was soft. She saw footprints. Small footprints, from a woman's shoes. They went to the door and back again. The rain did not come last night, so the footprints were still clear.

"Somebody visited you last night," Ellen said to the lighthouse. "And somebody had a key."

She walked back to town slowly. The sea was grey and the wind was getting stronger. She was thinking hard.

Chapter 5. The Man from London

The Harbour Hotel was the best hotel in Saltby. Richard Grant was sitting in the bar. He was about fifty, with a grey suit and a gold watch. He was reading a newspaper and drinking whisky. It was only three o'clock.

"Mr Grant?" said Ellen. "Can I talk to you?"

"Are you from the police?"

"Not exactly. I'm helping the police."

Mr Grant smiled. "Then sit down. I know why you're here. The map. And yes, I wanted to buy it. But I didn't steal it."

"How much did you offer Mrs Hale?"

"Five thousand pounds."

Ellen was surprised. "Only five thousand? Mrs Hale says it's worth eighty thousand."

Mr Grant laughed. "The real map is worth eighty thousand. Maybe a hundred. But the map in that museum is not the real map."

"How do you know?"

"I collect maps. It's my job and my hobby. I looked at it for one minute and I knew. It's a good copy. Maybe twenty years old. So I offered five thousand, because it's a nice copy. Mrs Hale was very angry. She asked me to leave."

"Did you tell anybody else about this?"

"Not yet." He finished his whisky. "But an expert from the British Museum is coming to Saltby next Friday. Did you know that? They want to put the Saltby map in a big exhibition in London. The expert is going to look at the map very carefully."

"How do you know that?"

"Everybody in the map world knows. It's a small world, like a small town."

Ellen stood up. "Thank you, Mr Grant. One more question. Where were you last night?"

"Here, in this bar, until midnight. Ask the barman. Then I went to bed. I'm too old to walk on beaches in the dark."

Ellen checked with the barman. It was true.

That evening, she called Sam. "Sam, I need some information. Who was the director of the museum twenty years ago?"

"I don't know. I can find out. Why?"

"Just a feeling," said Ellen.

An hour later, Sam called back. "It was Mrs Hale. She became director in 2003. And there's something else. In 2004, the museum had money problems. There was a big hole in the roof. Then, suddenly, Mrs Hale found the money. Twenty thousand pounds. She said it was from 'a private friend of the museum'."

"Interesting," said Ellen. "Very interesting."

"Mrs Marsh, what are you thinking?" asked Sam. "Please tell me. I'm the police officer, but I feel like the student."

Ellen laughed. "All right. Come to the shop. I'm going to make some coffee."

Ten minutes later, Sam was sitting in the old armchair in the bookshop. Ellen walked up and down between the shelves.

"Let's think about the facts," she said. "Fact one. The alarm was broken. Fact two. Nobody broke the door or the windows. So the thief had a key. Only Tom and Mrs Hale have keys."

"Yes," said Sam.

"Fact three. On Sunday, Tom didn't lock the door. Mrs Hale did. She stayed late, and that was unusual."

"Yes."

"Fact four. Lily and Mr Grant both say the map is a copy. Fact five. Twenty years ago, Mrs Hale suddenly found twenty thousand pounds. Fact six. An expert is coming on Friday."

Sam's eyes opened wide. "So the map wasn't stolen for money. It was stolen because it was a copy. Somebody didn't want the expert to see it."

"Exactly. If you steal a fake map, nobody can say it's fake."

"But this is all just ideas," said Sam. "We have no proof."

"Not yet," said Ellen. "But Lily saw a woman on the beach in a green coat. She was walking to the lighthouse and back. And I saw footprints at the lighthouse door. Tomorrow, we're going to visit Mrs Hale again. And we're going to look at her coat."

Chapter 6. The Missing Key

The next morning, it was raining. Ellen walked to the museum with Sam. Mrs Hale was in her office. She was wearing a long green coat.

"Is there any news?" she asked. "Did you find my map?"

"Not yet," said Ellen. "But I have some questions. Mrs Hale, on Sunday you stayed in the museum until after six. Why?"

"I had a lot of work."

"Tom says you locked the door. Not him."

"Yes, that's right. Is that a problem?"

"Did you come back later? At about eleven?"

"No. I was at home. I went to bed early."

Ellen looked at the green coat. At the bottom, there was a little line of dry sand.

"You have a lovely coat," she said. "Did you walk on the beach recently?"

Mrs Hale looked down at her coat. For a second, she said nothing.

"I walk on the beach every day," she said. "Everybody does."

"Of course," said Ellen. "Mrs Hale, did you know that Lily Carter thinks the map is a copy?"

"Lily is a young girl with a big imagination."

"Mr Grant thinks so too. And an expert from London is coming on Friday."

Mrs Hale stood up. "I don't like these questions. I think you should leave. Sam, please take her away. She isn't even a police officer now."

Sam looked at Ellen. Ellen smiled at Sam.

"Just one more thing," said Ellen. "Where is the lighthouse key?"

"What?"

"The old lighthouse. Nobody lives there now. The town council keeps it closed. But the museum has a key, I think. Because every summer you have a small exhibition there about the old lighthouse keepers. Am I right?"

Mrs Hale's face went white.

Ellen walked to a small box on the wall. Inside there were many keys with little labels. "Front door", "Garden", "Office". And one empty hook. The label under it said "Lighthouse".

"The key isn't here," said Ellen. "Where is it, Mrs Hale?"

Mrs Hale sat down slowly. She took off her glasses. Suddenly she looked very old and very tired.

"In my pocket," she said quietly.

Chapter 7. The Truth at the Lighthouse

They walked to the lighthouse together in the rain. Mrs Hale opened the old door with the key. Inside, it was dark and cold. There was a smell of salt and old wood.

At the bottom of the stairs, there was a plastic bag. Inside the bag, there was the map.

"I wanted to burn it," said Mrs Hale. "But I couldn't. It's a copy, but it's a beautiful copy. I paid an artist to make it in 2004."

"Tell us everything," said Sam gently.

Mrs Hale sat on the stairs. "Twenty years ago, the museum was dying. The roof was broken. The council had no money. Nobody cared. They wanted to close the museum and sell the building. I loved that museum. It was my whole life."

"So you sold the real map," said Ellen.

"Yes. To a private collector in Switzerland. He paid twenty thousand pounds. It was not a good price, but I needed the money quickly. I repaired the roof. I bought new lights. I saved the museum. And I put the copy on the wall. Nobody noticed for twenty years."

"Until this summer," said Ellen.

"Until the letter from the British Museum. I knew the expert was going to see the truth. Then Lily came with her drawing book, and that terrible man Grant. I was so frightened. I thought, if the map is stolen, nobody can check it. So I broke the glass, took the map, and walked along the beach in the dark. I wanted to hide it here forever."

"And the sand on the floor?" asked Ellen.

Mrs Hale laughed sadly. "I went back to the museum after the lighthouse. I wanted to check everything. I forgot about my shoes. Thirty years of reading crime novels, and I forgot about my shoes."

Nobody said anything for a while. The rain was loud on the windows.

"What happens now?" asked Mrs Hale.

"That's not my decision," said Ellen. "It's Sam's."

Sam looked at the map. Then at Mrs Hale. "I must report it, Mrs Hale. I'm sorry. But I'm going to tell them everything. About the roof, and the museum, and why you did it."

"Thank you, Sam," said Mrs Hale. Then she looked at Ellen. "How did you know? Really?"

"The sand was the first thing," said Ellen. "But the price was the important thing."

"The price?"

"When I asked about the map, you said eighty thousand pounds immediately. You didn't stop to think. But you didn't look sad. A director who loses the best thing in her museum is usually very upset. You weren't upset. You were frightened. Frightened people and sad people look different. I saw a lot of both in Bristol."

Mrs Hale nodded slowly. "You were a good detective."

"I still am, I think," said Ellen. "A little."

Two weeks later, there was a story in the local newspaper: "Museum Director Sold Famous Map". The police in Switzerland found the collector. He was very old now, and he was very surprised. In the end, he agreed to lend the real map to the British Museum for its exhibition. After that, it was going to come home to Saltby.

Mrs Hale did not go to prison. But she lost her job. The new director of the museum was Tom Bell. He was very proud. The first thing he did was repair the alarm.

Lily Carter finished her painting. Old Saltby and new Saltby, together. She gave it to the museum, and now it hangs in the main room, in the old wooden frame.

And Ellen went back to her bookshop. She drank her coffee, she sold her books and she walked by the sea in the evening.

One morning, Sam came into the shop again.

"Mrs Marsh," he said. "Somebody stole six lobsters from the fish market."

Ellen smiled and took her coat.

"Do you miss your old job?" Sam asked, as they walked down the street.

"No," said Ellen. "Not at all."

The End
