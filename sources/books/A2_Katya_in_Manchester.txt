Katya in Manchester

Chapter 1. The Offer

Katya Orlova was thirty-one years old. She lived in Samara, a big city on the Volga river. She worked as a software tester for a bank. Every day she checked other people's computer programs and found mistakes. She was very good at finding mistakes. In computer programs, and in life.

One evening in March, she got an email. It was from a company in Manchester, England. The company was called Wayfinder. It made travel apps for phones.

"Dear Ms Orlova," the email said. "Thank you for your interview last week. We are happy to offer you the job of Senior Tester. Can you start on the first of June?"

Katya read the email three times. Then she called her mother.

"Mama, I got the job. In England."

There was a long silence. "England? But it rains there all the time."

"It rains in Samara too, Mama."

"Not all the time. And what are you going to eat? Do they even have buckwheat?"

"I think I can live without buckwheat."

Her mother sighed. "You were always like this. When you were six, you wanted to fly to the moon."

"England is closer than the moon," said Katya.

Her best friend Olya was more excited. They met in their usual cafe.

"Manchester! Football! Rock music! English men with nice accents!"

"I'm going there to work, Olya. Not to find a husband."

"Of course," said Olya. "But you can do two things at the same time."

Katya laughed. But that night she couldn't sleep. She looked at the ceiling and thought about all the things that could go wrong. She didn't know anybody in Manchester. Her English was good, but she learned it from books and films. What if people didn't understand her? What if she didn't understand them?

At three in the morning, she got up, opened her laptop and wrote a reply.

"Dear Wayfinder, thank you very much. I accept. See you on the first of June."

Then she slept like a baby.

Chapter 2. Flat 3B

Katya arrived in Manchester on a Saturday at the end of May. It was raining. Of course it was raining.

She took a taxi from the airport. The driver talked all the time, but Katya understood only about half of his words.

"You alright, love? First time in Manchester? You'll love it. Best city in the world, this."

"Yes," said Katya. "Thank you. Very nice."

She hoped that was the right answer. The driver seemed happy.

Her new flat was in an old red brick building near the city centre. The owner, Mrs Doyle, was waiting at the door. She was a small woman of about seventy with bright pink hair.

"You must be Katya! Come in, come in, you're wet. Do you want a cup of tea?"

"Yes, please," said Katya.

In the next three hours, Mrs Doyle made her four cups of tea. Katya learned that in England, tea is not just a drink. It's the answer to every problem. Are you wet? Tea. Are you tired? Tea. Is your new life very frightening? Definitely tea.

Flat 3B was on the top floor. It was small, with one bedroom, a tiny kitchen and a window with a view of a hundred other red roofs. Katya loved it immediately.

"The heating is a bit strange," said Mrs Doyle. "You must hit the box twice before you turn it on. And the shower is either very hot or very cold. Nothing in between. Oh, and there's Mr Biscuit."

"Who is Mr Biscuit?"

"My cat. He's orange and very fat. He visits everybody in the building. Don't give him food. He's on a diet."

"Okay," said Katya.

"And Ben in 2B plays the trumpet. Only in the evenings. He's learning. He's... not very good yet."

"Okay," said Katya again. She was beginning to worry.

That night, she unpacked her two suitcases. At nine o'clock, a terrible sound came from the floor below. It was like a sad elephant. It was Ben's trumpet. He was playing "Happy Birthday". Badly. Very badly.

Katya put her head under the pillow. "Welcome to England," she said to herself.

On Sunday, she went to the supermarket. It was huge. There were forty kinds of bread, but she couldn't find any black bread. There were a hundred kinds of crisps, with very strange flavours. Prawn cocktail. Roast chicken. Salt and vinegar. She bought the salt and vinegar ones. They were so sour that her eyes watered.

At the cash desk, the young man said, "Do you need a bag?"

"Yes, please."

"Are you okay there, love?"

Katya was confused. Why did everybody call her "love"? The taxi driver, Mrs Doyle, and now this boy. In Russia, only your mother or your boyfriend says that.

Later, she asked Mrs Doyle.

"Oh, it means nothing, dear," said Mrs Doyle. "In Manchester everybody is 'love'. The bus driver, the postman, the doctor. Don't marry them because of it."

Chapter 3. The Tea Round

On Monday, Katya started her new job. The Wayfinder office was in an old factory. Now it had big windows, plants everywhere and a table for table tennis. About forty people worked there. They all wore jeans and trainers. Katya wore her best grey suit. She felt like a teacher at a student party.

Her new boss was a man called Gareth. He was tall, with a red beard, and he was from Wales.

"Katya! Welcome! Lovely to meet you. Let me show you around."

He introduced her to everybody. Katya tried to remember the names. Priya, Josh, another Josh, Sandra, Femi, Big Dave, Little Dave...

"Why is he Little Dave?" Katya asked quietly. "He is taller than Big Dave."

"That's the joke," said Gareth.

Katya did not understand the joke. She decided to think about it later.

In the afternoon, there was a team meeting. Josh showed a new design for an app. It had bright purple buttons and very small letters.

"So," said Gareth. "What does everybody think?"

"Interesting," said Sandra.

"Quite nice," said Femi.

"I'll think about it," said Big Dave.

"It is bad," said Katya. "The letters are too small. Old people cannot read them. And purple buttons on a purple background are a mistake. Nobody can see them."

The room went very quiet. Josh looked at the table. Katya's face went hot. In Samara, this was a normal meeting. Here, it seemed, it was not.

Then Gareth started laughing. "Thank you, Katya. Finally, somebody said it."

After the meeting, Josh came to her desk. "You're right about the buttons," he said. "I knew it, really. But next time, maybe say 'I have a small idea' first. That's the English way."

"I have a small idea," Katya repeated. She wrote it down in her notebook. It was the first sentence on a long list.

At eleven o'clock, Priya stood up. "Tea round! Who wants a brew?"

Katya learned later that "a brew" means a cup of tea. Everybody shouted something. "Milk, no sugar!" "Black, two sugars!" "Green tea for me!" Priya wrote it all down. Then she disappeared into the kitchen. Ten minutes later, she came back with twelve cups.

"In this office, we do a tea round," explained Gareth. "Somebody makes tea for everybody. Then next time, somebody else does it."

On Wednesday, it was Katya's turn. She was very nervous. She wrote down every order very carefully. Then she made the tea in the Russian way. Strong, in a big pot, with lemon. She did not put milk in any cup. Milk in tea seemed strange to her.

She carried the cups back to the team. Everybody looked at their tea. Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ooh," said Sandra finally. "That's... interesting. Very... Russian."

"Is it bad?" asked Katya.

"No, no," said Josh. "It's not bad at all."

Later, Priya explained. "In England, 'not bad at all' can mean many things. It can mean 'good'. It can mean 'terrible'. You must listen to the voice."

"How was Josh's voice?"

Priya smiled. "Terrible."

Katya laughed. It was the first time she laughed in Manchester. It felt good.

Chapter 4. Mr Biscuit

One evening in June, Katya came home from work and opened her door. There was an orange cat on her sofa. He was very big, and he was sleeping. He looked like a pumpkin with legs.

"You must be Mr Biscuit," said Katya.

The cat opened one eye. Then he closed it again.

"How did you get in?"

She looked around. The kitchen window was open. There was a small roof outside, and from the roof the cat could walk to every flat in the building.

Katya decided not to fight. She made dinner. Mr Biscuit woke up very quickly when he smelled the chicken.

"No," said Katya. "You're on a diet."

Mr Biscuit looked at her with big sad eyes. He looked very hungry and very lonely. Katya felt the same.

"Just a little piece," she said. "Don't tell Mrs Doyle."

After that, Mr Biscuit came every evening. He sat on the sofa while Katya watched films. He listened while she talked about her day. He was the perfect friend. He never interrupted, and he never said "not bad at all".

Life was slowly getting easier. Katya learned the bus routes. She learned that "cheers" means "thank you" and also "goodbye". She learned that people say "sorry" all the time, even when you step on their foot. She bought an umbrella, then another umbrella, because the wind broke the first one.

But the evenings were hard. Her colleagues were friendly at work, but after six o'clock they went home to their families and friends. Katya went home to an orange cat and a sad trumpet.

On Friday, she called Olya.

"So? Did you meet a nice Englishman?"

"I met a fat cat," said Katya. "And the man downstairs plays the trumpet like a dying cow."

"Is he handsome?"

"I don't know. I never saw him. I only hear him."

"Then go and see him," said Olya. "What are you waiting for?"

Chapter 5. The Man with the Trumpet

On Saturday morning, there was a knock at Katya's door. A man was standing there. He was about thirty-five, with dark curly hair and kind brown eyes. He was holding a plate of biscuits.

"Hi. Sorry to bother you. I'm Ben, from 2B. I live downstairs."

"Ah," said Katya. "The trumpet."

Ben's face went red. "Yes. Sorry about that. I wanted to say sorry, actually. That's why I brought biscuits. I know I'm terrible."

"You are not terrible," said Katya. Then she remembered Priya's lesson. "Okay. You are a little bit terrible."

Ben laughed. He had a nice laugh. "Thank you for being honest. Nobody in England is honest."

"Why do you play the trumpet?"

"My grandfather played in a jazz band. He gave me his trumpet before he died. I promised him I was going to learn. That was two years ago." He smiled. "I'm a slow learner."

Katya invited him in. They drank tea and ate the biscuits. Mr Biscuit came in through the window and sat on Ben's knees.

"He likes you," said Katya.

"He likes everybody with food," said Ben. "He comes to my flat too. He eats my toast."

They talked for two hours. Ben was a nurse at a children's hospital. He worked long hours, often at night. He played the trumpet to relax after work. He was from Leeds, and he moved to Manchester five years ago.

"I remember my first months here," he said. "I felt so lonely. I didn't know anybody. I went to work, then home, then work again."

"Yes," said Katya quietly. "That's my life now."

Ben looked at her. "Well, then. Do you like quizzes?"

"Quizzes?"

"In the pub on the corner, every Thursday, there's a quiz night. Teams answer questions about history, music, sport, everything. My team is terrible. We always lose. We need a clever person."

"How do you know I'm clever?"

"You're a software tester. You find mistakes. That's clever."

Katya smiled. "Okay. Thursday."

Chapter 6. A Very Bad Week

The next week was the worst week of Katya's life in England.

On Monday, she missed her bus and was late for an important meeting. On Tuesday, the shower decided to be very cold. Only very cold. On Wednesday, it rained so much that her shoes were wet all day.

And on Thursday morning, there was a big problem at work.

Wayfinder had a new app. It helped tourists find restaurants in Paris. Katya tested it for two weeks. She said it was ready. On Thursday, it went live. And at ten o'clock, the phones started ringing.

The app was sending people to the wrong places. A family from Japan wanted a French restaurant. The app sent them to a dentist. A couple from Canada wanted a romantic dinner. The app sent them to a car park.

Gareth called Katya into his office. "What happened?"

Katya looked at the computer. She found the problem in twenty minutes. It was a small mistake in the maps. And it was her mistake. She didn't check one small part of the program.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's my fault. I missed it."

Gareth was quiet. Then he said, "Okay. Fix it. Quickly."

She worked until nine o'clock at night. She fixed the problem. But she felt terrible. She was the person who finds mistakes. And she made a big mistake.

She forgot about the quiz.

At home, she sat on the floor of her kitchen and cried. Mr Biscuit came and sat next to her. Then she opened her laptop and looked at flights to Samara.

There was a flight on Saturday.

There was a knock at the door. It was Ben.

"You didn't come to the quiz," he said. Then he saw her face. "Hey. What's wrong?"

Katya told him everything. The bus, the shower, the shoes, the app, the dentist and the car park.

Ben didn't laugh. Well, he laughed a little at the dentist. But then he made her a cup of tea and sat with her on the kitchen floor.

"Can I tell you something?" he said. "Last year, at the hospital, I gave a child the wrong medicine. It wasn't dangerous, luckily. But I felt like the worst nurse in the world. I wanted to leave."

"What happened?"

"My boss said, 'Ben, everybody makes mistakes. Good people learn from them. Bad people hide them.' You didn't hide your mistake. You fixed it. That's good."

Katya closed her laptop. "Maybe I'm not going to buy that ticket."

"What ticket?"

"Nothing," said Katya. "It's not important now."

Chapter 7. Quiz Night

The next morning at work, Gareth came to Katya's desk.

"Katya, about yesterday."

She felt sick. "Yes?"

"You found the problem in twenty minutes. You fixed it in one evening. Our last tester needed three days for a problem like that. And then he went on holiday." He smiled under his red beard. "Also, the Japanese family went to the dentist, and the dentist recommended a great restaurant. They wrote a very nice review. So, not bad at all."

"Is that good 'not bad at all' or bad 'not bad at all'?"

Gareth laughed. "Good. Definitely good. And Katya? You can stop wearing that suit. You make us all look lazy."

The next Thursday, Katya went to the quiz. Ben's team was called "The Losers". There was Ben, his friend Marcus, and Marcus's girlfriend Jo. They welcomed Katya with a lot of noise.

The pub was warm and busy. The quiz master was an old man with a microphone and a very loud voice.

"Question one. What is the capital city of Australia?"

"Sydney," said Marcus immediately.

"Canberra," said Katya.

Everybody looked at her. Ben wrote "Canberra".

It continued like this. Katya knew about geography, science and history. Marcus knew about football. Jo knew about films. Ben knew about music, which was a surprise to nobody except Katya.

The last question was worth five points.

"What is the longest river in Europe?"

The other teams looked confused. Some wrote "the Danube". Some wrote "the Rhine".

Katya smiled. "The Volga," she said. "I grew up next to it."

The Losers won by two points. It was the first time in four years. Marcus stood on a chair and sang. Jo hugged Katya. The prize was a box of chocolates and a bottle of cheap wine. Ben said it was the best prize in the world.

Later, Ben walked Katya home. It was not raining, for once. The stars were out over the red roofs.

"Thank you," said Katya. "For the quiz. For last week. For everything."

"Thank you," said Ben. "For the Volga."

They stood at the door of the building. Neither of them wanted to go inside.

"Katya," said Ben. "Would you like to have dinner with me? Not at a quiz. Just dinner. Just us."

Katya thought about it. For about one second.

"Yes," she said. "But I choose the restaurant. Not the app."

On Saturday, they went to a small Italian restaurant near the canal. Katya wore a blue dress. Ben wore a shirt with no trumpet on it. It was the first time she saw him without his hospital clothes or an old jumper.

The dinner did not start perfectly. Ben knocked over a glass of water. Katya ordered "pasta with seafood", but her English was too fast, and the waiter brought pizza with pineapple.

"Is this an English tradition?" she asked, looking at the pizza.

"No," said Ben. "This is a crime. In Italy, they put people in prison for this."

They laughed so much that the people at the next table looked at them. After that, everything was easy. They talked about Samara and Leeds, about their families, about their jobs. Ben told her about the children at the hospital. Katya told him about her mother and the buckwheat.

"Your mother sounds wonderful," said Ben.

"She is wonderful. And she worries about everything. She is going to ask about you. Many questions."

"What are you going to tell her?"

Katya thought for a moment. "I am going to tell her that you are kind. And that you play the trumpet very badly."

"That's fair," said Ben.

At the end of the evening, he walked her home again. At the door, he kissed her. It was a short, shy kiss. Upstairs, in Flat 3B, Mr Biscuit was watching from the window.

Chapter 8. Home

The summer passed quickly, and then the autumn. Katya stopped wearing her grey suit. She started to drink tea with milk, but only at work, and only to be polite. She learned to say "I have a small idea" before every big idea. She joined the office table tennis team and beat Big Dave three times. Little Dave was very happy about that.

Ben's trumpet got a little better. Only a little. But now, when Katya heard it in the evening, she smiled.

It was December now. It was snowing in Manchester. Not much, just a little, but everybody in the city was very excited. In Samara, this was nothing. But Katya was excited too.

She was in Flat 3B, cooking. The little kitchen was hot and full of steam. She was making pelmeni, Russian dumplings, for twelve people. Her mother sent the recipe by email, with many instructions and three warnings.

Tonight there was a party. Her first party in her new home.

The guests came at seven. Gareth and his wife. Priya with a bottle of wine. Both Joshes. Big Dave and Little Dave. Marcus and Jo. Mrs Doyle, with her hair now green for Christmas. And Ben, of course. Ben came early to help, but mostly he ate the pelmeni when Katya wasn't looking.

The flat was too small for so many people. Some people sat on the floor. Little Dave sat on the windowsill. Mr Biscuit sat on the table, and nobody could move him.

"These are amazing," said Priya, with her mouth full. "What's in them?"

"Meat, onion and love," said Katya. "And a lot of time. My mother says you must make them with friends. So next year, you are all going to help me."

"Next year?" said Gareth. "So you're staying?"

Katya looked around the room. At her colleagues, who were now her friends. At Mrs Doyle, who was teaching Josh how to dance. At Mr Biscuit, who was trying to steal a dumpling from Big Dave. At Ben, who was smiling at her across the room.

"Yes," she said. "I'm staying."

Later, when everybody was singing, Katya went into the kitchen and called her mother.

"Mama, it's snowing here."

"Snowing? In England? Are you warm? Do you have a good coat?"

"I have a good coat. And I have good friends. And Mama... I think I'm happy."

Her mother was quiet for a moment. "Is this because of the trumpet man?"

Katya laughed. "Partly."

"Is he good at the trumpet now?"

From the living room came a sound. It was Ben. He was playing "Happy Birthday" for Mrs Doyle, who was seventy-one today. And it was... not bad. Not bad at all.

"Yes, Mama," said Katya. "He is getting better."

The End
