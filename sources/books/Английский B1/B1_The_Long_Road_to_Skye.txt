The Long Road to Skye

Chapter 1. A Call After Twenty Years

Alan Turner was sixty-four years old, and he liked his life to be quiet and organised. He had been a geography teacher in Leeds for thirty-eight years. He had retired two years ago, in the same year that his wife Helen had died. Since then, his days had all looked the same. He got up at seven, read the newspaper, walked to the park, ate lunch at twelve, watched the news at six and went to bed at ten.

So when the phone rang at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night in June, he was already in his pyjamas, and he was almost angry.

"Hello?"

"Alan! Alan Turner! It's me!"

Alan didn't say anything. He knew the voice immediately, although he hadn't heard it for twenty years. It was loud, warm and Scottish, and it belonged to the only person in the world who would call at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night without saying sorry.

"Dougie?"

"The very same! How are you, old friend?"

Alan and Dougie McLeod had met at university in Edinburgh in 1979. They had shared a cold, terrible flat for three years. Alan had studied hard. Dougie had played the fiddle in pubs, fallen in love every week and somehow passed his exams at the last minute. After university, they had stayed close for many years. Then Dougie had moved to London, life had become busy, and slowly, without any argument, they had stopped calling each other.

"I'm fine," Alan said. "Dougie, it's eleven o'clock."

"Is it? Sorry, sorry. Listen, Alan. I need a favour. A big one."

"How big?"

"I need you to drive me to the Isle of Skye."

Alan sat down on the edge of his bed. "The Isle of Skye. In Scotland."

"That's the one. Big island, lots of rain, very beautiful. You must remember it. We went there in 1981, with that terrible tent."

"Why can't you drive yourself?"

There was a short pause. "I lost my licence. It's a long story. And I don't think I should take the train. I need a friend with me for this, Alan. I really do."

Something in his voice had changed. The jokes had gone, and underneath there was something nervous, almost frightened.

"What's on Skye, Dougie?"

"My daughter. Kirsty. She lives in a little village called Elgol. She's going to have a baby next month. My first grandchild." He laughed, but it wasn't a happy laugh. "And she hasn't spoken to me for eleven years."

Alan looked around his tidy bedroom. Helen's photo was on the table by the bed. She had always liked Dougie. "Life's too short, Alan," she used to say. "Phone him."

"When do you want to leave?" Alan heard himself say.

"Is Wednesday too soon?"

Chapter 2. The Pink Car

On Wednesday morning, Alan discovered that his own car had a problem with its brakes. The garage said it would take a week to repair.

"No problem," said Dougie, who had arrived on the train from London the night before with one small bag, a fiddle case and a large bottle of whisky. "We'll rent one."

The rental company near Leeds station had only one car left. It was very small and very pink. On the side, in big white letters, were the words "PRINCESS PARTY HIRE - Make Every Day Magic!"

"It belonged to a company that does children's parties," the young man at the desk explained. "They gave it back last week. It's a good car. Very economical."

Alan looked at the car. Then he looked at Dougie, who was already laughing so hard that he had to hold on to a wall.

"We are not driving four hundred miles in that," Alan said.

"Alan," Dougie said, wiping his eyes. "We are absolutely driving four hundred miles in that."

They put their bags in the tiny boot. The fiddle case had to go on the back seat. Dougie's knees were almost touching his chin. Alan, who was tall, had to drive with his head bent slightly to one side.

Alan had planned the trip carefully. He had printed maps, even though the car had satnav. He had booked two hotels: one in Glasgow for the first night and one in Fort William for the second. He had made a list of petrol stations. He had made sandwiches.

Dougie had brought the whisky.

For the first hour, the journey went well. They drove north on the motorway, and they talked about the old days: their terrible flat, the landlady who hated music, the night Dougie had played the fiddle on the roof of the university library. They laughed a lot. Alan realised he hadn't laughed like this since Helen had died.

Then Dougie said, "Do you mind if I change the music?" and pressed a button on the dashboard.

The car was immediately filled with a song from a children's film, very loud. A girl's voice was singing about rainbows and dreams. Dougie pressed another button. The song became louder. He pressed all the buttons. The windscreen wipers started, the lights came on, and the song continued.

"How do you turn it off?" Dougie shouted.

"I don't know! It's your fault!"

It was impossible to turn it off. They tried everything. In the end, they drove for two hundred miles, all the way to Glasgow, listening to the same twelve songs about princesses, again and again. By the time they reached the hotel, Dougie knew all the words. He sang them at dinner.

People looked at them. Alan, to his own surprise, didn't care.

Chapter 3. Lost in Glasgow

The next morning, Dougie typed their destination into the satnav, because Alan was paying for breakfast.

"Fort William," he said. "Easy."

Forty minutes later, they were in a car park behind a supermarket in the south of Glasgow. The satnav was saying, in a cheerful voice, "You have reached your destination."

Alan looked at the screen. "Dougie. You typed 'Fort William Street'."

"Did I? Well, there are so many Fort Williams."

"There is one Fort William, Dougie. It's a town. It's in the Highlands. It has a mountain next to it."

"Then this must be a different one."

They argued for ten minutes in the supermarket car park. Alan said Dougie had never been able to concentrate on anything. Dougie said Alan had always been too serious, even at nineteen. Alan said at least he had been able to keep a driving licence. That was a mistake. Dougie went quiet and looked out of the window.

"Sorry," Alan said, after a while. "That wasn't fair."

"No. It was fair." Dougie sighed. "I lost it because I drove after drinking. Three years ago. I didn't hit anybody, thank God. But I could have." He turned the bottle of whisky in his hands. "I haven't had a drink since then, Alan. This bottle is for Kirsty's husband. A present. I've never even met him."

Alan didn't know what to say. So he said nothing, and after a minute he put the car into first gear.

"Right," he said. "Let's find the real Fort William."

The road north from Glasgow went along the side of Loch Lomond. The water was silver and calm, and the hills on the other side were green and purple. Then the land became higher and emptier, and the road went through Glencoe. Huge dark mountains stood on both sides, with white waterfalls running down them like lines on an old man's face.

Dougie had stopped joking. He was looking at the mountains with his mouth slightly open.

"I'd forgotten," he said quietly. "I'd forgotten how big it all is. You live in London for thirty years, and you forget that the world is bigger than you."

Just then, the car made a strange noise. It was a kind of cough. Then another one. Then the engine stopped completely, and they rolled slowly to the side of the road in the middle of Glencoe, with no other cars in sight.

The princess music, of course, continued.

Chapter 4. Mr MacPhail and the Cows

There was no phone signal in the valley. They stood next to the pink car in the wind, looking up and down the empty road.

"Did you check the petrol?" Dougie asked.

"Of course I checked the petrol! I made a list of petrol stations!" Alan looked at the dashboard through the window. The petrol light was on. It had probably been on for a long time, but it was a small pink light on a pink dashboard, and he hadn't noticed it.

"Ah," said Alan.

"Ah," agreed Dougie, and he started to laugh.

They waited for almost an hour. Three cars passed and didn't stop. Perhaps, Alan thought, people didn't trust two old men standing next to a car that said "Make Every Day Magic!"

Finally, an old Land Rover came round the corner and stopped. The driver was a small, thin man of about eighty, with a white beard and a dog on the seat next to him.

"Having a bit of trouble?" he asked.

His name was Mr MacPhail, and he had a farm a few miles further along the valley. He had a can of petrol in the back of the Land Rover, "for emergencies", and he gave them enough to reach the next town. He refused to take any money.

"But you can do something for me," he said, looking at the fiddle case on the back seat. "Is that a fiddle?"

"It is," said Dougie.

"Can you play it?"

"I used to play it quite well."

Mr MacPhail's eyes shone. "My wife loved the fiddle. She died in the spring. Would you play something for her? Just one tune. She's buried just up there, by the church."

So they followed the Land Rover to a small white church on the side of a hill. The wind had dropped, and the evening light was golden on the mountains. Dougie took out his fiddle, tuned it and, standing next to a simple grave, played a slow Scottish song that Alan didn't know. It was the most beautiful thing Alan had heard in years. Mr MacPhail stood with his hat in his hands and tears on his cheeks. Even the dog sat still.

When it was finished, nobody spoke for a long time.

Then a group of large, hairy, orange Highland cows walked slowly round the corner of the church, looked at them, and started to eat the flowers on the next grave.

"Ach, not again," said Mr MacPhail, and he ran after them, waving his hat.

Chapter 5. A Night by the Loch

They reached Fort William at nine in the evening. The town was full of people in walking boots, because the next day there was a big mountain race. The hotel receptionist looked at her computer and shook her head.

"I'm very sorry, Mr Turner. Your booking is for next Thursday, not this Thursday."

Alan stared at her. He had checked the booking three times. He was sure of it. He took out his printed confirmation. It said next Thursday.

"Every room in town is full," the receptionist said kindly. "Because of the race, you see."

Dougie put a hand on Alan's shoulder. "Don't worry, old friend. We've slept in worse places. Remember the tent in 1981?"

"The tent in 1981 fell down in the middle of the night, and we slept in a bus shelter."

"Exactly. So this will be better."

They bought fish and chips and drove a few miles out of town until they found a quiet place beside a loch. The water was perfectly still. The mountains were reflected in it, upside down, and at ten o'clock at night the sky was still light, because in the north of Scotland in June it never gets really dark.

It was beautiful for about four minutes. Then the midges arrived.

Midges are tiny flying insects, much smaller than mosquitoes, and in summer they live everywhere in the west of Scotland. One midge is nothing. But midges never come alone. They came in their thousands, in clouds, and they bit every part of the two men that wasn't covered by clothes.

They ran back into the car and closed the doors and windows. Through the glass, they could see the midges waiting for them, like a grey fog.

"Well," said Dougie, scratching his neck. "Welcome to Scotland."

So they slept, or tried to sleep, in the tiny pink car, with the seats pushed back as far as they would go, which was not very far. Neither of them could sleep. And in the strange half-light, with their faces hidden from each other, it became easier to talk.

"Why did Kirsty stop speaking to you?" Alan asked.

For a long time, Dougie didn't answer. Then he said, "Her mother, Jean, and I split up when Kirsty was twelve. That was my fault. I was always on tour with the band, always in pubs. Then, eleven years ago, Jean got sick. Cancer. Kirsty called me and asked me to come. And I said I would come. I promised." He stopped. "I was in a bar in Glasgow on the day of the funeral. I couldn't face it. I just couldn't. I missed it, Alan. I missed my wife's funeral, and I left my daughter standing there alone."

Alan closed his eyes. He thought about Helen's funeral, and how difficult it had been to stand up and walk into the church. He understood, a little. He didn't think it was right. But he understood.

"I've written to her every year on her birthday," Dougie continued. "She's never answered. Then, a month ago, she sent me a card. Just one line. 'You're going to be a grandfather in July.' No address, just the postmark from Elgol. I don't even know if it means she wants to see me."

"She sent the card," Alan said. "She didn't have to."

"No," Dougie said quietly. "She didn't have to."

Outside, the midges danced against the window. Somewhere across the loch, a bird was calling. After a while, Alan said, "I haven't told anyone this. But since Helen died, there are some weeks when I don't speak to anybody at all. Not a word. Except the woman in the shop."

"Then it's good I phoned you," said Dougie.

"Yes," said Alan. "It's good you phoned me."

Chapter 6. The Wrong Ferry

In the morning, they were both tired, bitten and stiff, but they were in a good mood. Alan had decided, during the night, that they would take the ferry from Mallaig to Skye, not the bridge.

"It's more romantic," he explained. "Arriving on an island by boat. That's how we did it in 1981."

The ferry left Mallaig at 11:30. They arrived at 11:05, which, Alan said proudly, was perfect timing. Then Dougie said, "Wait, I just need to buy something," and disappeared into a small shop on the harbour.

At 11:20, he hadn't come back. At 11:25, Alan went to find him. Dougie was standing in the middle of the shop, holding two tiny knitted hats, one yellow and one green.

"Which one?" he asked seriously. "We don't know if it's a boy or a girl. Yellow is safer. But green is the colour of the Highlands."

"Dougie, the ferry!"

"This is important, Alan!"

They bought both hats. They ran out of the shop. The ferry was moving slowly away from the harbour. A man in a yellow jacket waved at them cheerfully from the back of it.

The next ferry was at four o'clock. Alan sat down on a wall and put his head in his hands.

"The bridge is only two hours away," Dougie said. "We can drive round."

"That's not the point. I had a plan."

"Alan, my friend, when has any plan worked on this trip?"

Alan opened his mouth to argue. Then he closed it again. He thought about the pink car, the princess songs, Fort William Street, the petrol, the hotel, the midges. And slowly, for the first time in two years, he began to laugh at himself. He laughed until he couldn't breathe, sitting on a wall in Mallaig harbour, while seagulls watched him and Dougie held two baby hats.

They drove round to the bridge. As they crossed it, the sun came out, and the whole island of Skye appeared in front of them, green and wild, with the dark Cuillin mountains rising in the distance like the teeth of some enormous animal.

"There it is," said Dougie, and he went very quiet.

Chapter 7. The Ceilidh

It was too late to drive to Elgol that evening, so they stopped in a village called Broadford. This time, Alan didn't try to book anything. They simply knocked on the door of a small guest house with a sign that said "Rooms". The owner, a cheerful woman called Morag, had one room left, with two narrow beds.

"There's a ceilidh at the village hall tonight," she told them. "You should come. Everyone will be there."

A ceilidh is a traditional Scottish party with music and dancing. Alan had not danced since his wedding. He tried to say no, but Morag wasn't the kind of person who accepted no as an answer.

The village hall was hot, noisy and full of people of every age, from small children to old farmers. A band of three young musicians was playing at the front: a guitar, an accordion and a fiddle. People were dancing in long lines, turning, clapping and laughing when they made mistakes.

Morag took Alan's hand and pulled him into a dance before he could escape. He was terrible at it. He went left when everyone else went right. He stepped on her feet twice. But she just laughed and showed him again, and by the third dance he was beginning to understand the steps. His face was red, and his shirt was wet, and he was, he realised with surprise, happy.

Then the young fiddle player saw Dougie's fiddle case, which he had brought with him, as always.

"Do you play?" she called. "Come up! Give us a tune!"

The whole room turned and looked at him. Dougie shook his head and said no, but the room began to clap and shout, and in the end he walked slowly to the front.

He played for an hour. Fast tunes and slow ones, old songs that the old farmers sang along to, and new ones that made the young people dance until they were exhausted. Alan watched his friend and saw him become twenty years old again, his eyes shining, his foot hitting the floor in time with the music.

Later, when they were walking back to the guest house under a sky that was still pale blue at midnight, Dougie suddenly stopped.

"I can't do it, Alan," he said. "Tomorrow. I can't go to her door."

"What are you talking about?"

"Tonight, everyone liked me. Everyone was happy to see me. Tomorrow, she'll look at me, and she'll see the man who didn't come to her mother's funeral. She's going to close the door in my face. And she'll be right to do it."

"Maybe she will," Alan said. "And maybe she won't. But if you don't knock, you'll never know. You'll just be a man who drove four hundred miles in a pink car to sit outside his daughter's house."

Dougie didn't answer. But in the morning, when Alan woke up, Dougie was already dressed, sitting on the end of his bed, holding the two little hats.

"Come on, then," he said. "Before I change my mind."

Chapter 8. The Last Mile

The road to Elgol was a single-track road. That means it was only wide enough for one car, with small "passing places" every few hundred metres where you could pull in and let other cars go by. It went up and down over the hills and round the edge of a long loch, and it had more sheep on it than cars.

The sheep did not care about passing places. They stood in the middle of the road and looked at the pink car with their stupid, calm faces, and they moved only when they wanted to.

"Go on," said Alan, pressing the horn gently. "Move. Please."

The horn did not make a normal sound. It played the first line of a princess song.

The sheep looked at each other and walked slowly away. Dougie laughed so much that he had to wipe his eyes. Alan noticed that his hands were shaking.

The weather had changed again. Grey clouds had come in from the sea, and soon it was raining hard. The windscreen wipers could hardly keep the glass clear. About a mile before Elgol, as they were going down a steep hill, a large white van came round the corner towards them, much too fast. Alan pulled hard to the left. The little pink car went off the road, bumped across the wet grass and stopped, with a soft, sad sound, in a ditch full of water.

For a moment, they sat there in silence, listening to the rain on the roof. The van had already disappeared.

"Are you all right?" Alan asked.

"I'm fine. Are you?"

"I'm fine." Alan tried to drive forward, then backwards. The wheels just turned round and round in the mud. They were stuck.

There was still no phone signal. Dougie got out and stood in the rain. Water was running down his face and into his collar. He looked down the hill towards the small group of white houses by the sea.

"It's not far," he said. "We can walk."

"Dougie, it's raining like the end of the world."

"I've waited eleven years, Alan. I'm not waiting for the rain to stop."

So they walked. Dougie carried the fiddle case and the bag with the little hats and the bottle of whisky. Alan carried nothing, because he had forgotten his coat in Leeds, and he was too busy being cold and wet. Their shoes filled with water. The wind blew the rain sideways into their faces.

At the bottom of the hill, a woman was standing outside the village shop. Dougie stopped her and asked if she knew where Kirsty McLeod lived. The woman looked at him for a long time, at his wet grey hair and his fiddle case.

"Kirsty Mackinnon now," she said. "She married Callum Mackinnon. The blue door at the end, by the harbour." She paused. "You're her dad, aren't you? You have the same eyes."

Dougie nodded. He couldn't speak.

The blue door was at the end of a row of small houses facing the sea. There were children's toys in the front garden and a boat on the beach outside. Dougie stood in front of the door for a full minute. Alan stood behind him and didn't say anything. There was nothing he could say.

Then Dougie lifted his hand and knocked.

Chapter 9. The Blue Door

The door was opened by a young woman of about thirty-five. She had red hair tied back from her face, and she was very pregnant. She had Dougie's eyes. She looked at her father, standing in the rain with water running off his nose, and her face went completely white.

For a long moment, nobody said anything. The only sound was the rain and the sea.

"Hello, Kirsty," Dougie said finally. His voice was very small.

"What are you doing here?"

"You sent me a card."

"I sent you a card. I didn't send you an invitation." Her voice was hard, but her hand was holding the door frame tightly, as if she needed it to stand up. "Eleven years, Dad. Eleven years. And you just appear at my door?"

"I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Kirsty. I know sorry isn't enough. I should have been there. For your mum. For you. I was a coward, and I've hated myself every day since." He held out the bag. "I brought you... I brought these. For the baby. I didn't know which colour."

Kirsty looked down at the bag. She took out the two little knitted hats, the yellow and the green, and held them in her hands. Her face changed, and Alan saw her chin begin to shake.

Then a tall man with a dark beard appeared behind her in the hall. He looked at Dougie and at Alan, then at his wife.

"Kirsty? Who is it?"

"It's my dad," she said, without turning round.

The man, who must have been Callum, looked at the two wet old men on his doorstep. Then he did a very practical thing. "Well, he can't stand there in the rain," he said. "None of us can. Come in, both of you, before you die of cold. I'll put the kettle on."

The house was small and warm and smelled of wood smoke. Callum gave them towels and dry jumpers, which were much too big for Dougie and much too short for Alan. They sat at the kitchen table, and for the first half hour the conversation was very careful and very polite, about the weather, and the road, and the drive from Leeds. Alan told the story of the pink car, and Callum laughed loudly. Kirsty didn't laugh. But Alan saw her mouth move a little at the corners.

Then Kirsty looked at the fiddle case by the door.

"You still play," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Every day."

She was silent for a moment. "When I was small, you used to play me a song before I went to sleep. I've forgotten the name. Mum used to say it was the only time you were ever on time for anything."

Dougie looked at her. Then he stood up, took out the fiddle and, standing in his daughter's kitchen in a borrowed jumper, began to play. It was a simple, slow tune, gentle as a cradle. Halfway through, Kirsty put her hand over her mouth. By the end, she was crying, and so was Dougie, and Callum was looking out of the window very hard, and Alan had to go into the hall for a moment to look at a picture on the wall that he couldn't actually see.

When he came back, Kirsty and her father were holding each other in the middle of the kitchen, and neither of them was saying anything at all.

Chapter 10. Home

Callum and a neighbour pulled the pink car out of the ditch the next morning with a tractor. It was muddy and had a small dent in the door, but it still worked. The music still played. Callum thought it was the funniest thing he had ever seen, and he took photos of it from every side.

Dougie did not come back to Leeds. Kirsty had asked him to stay until the baby was born. "Not because I've forgiven you," she said. "I'm not sure I have, yet. But because I want my child to know who you are. And because somebody needs to play the fiddle at the christening."

Alan stayed for three more days. He walked on the beach in the mornings and helped Callum paint the boat. In the evenings, he sat by the fire and listened to Dougie and Kirsty talk, carefully at first, and then more easily, about Jean, and about the years they had lost. On the third evening, he drove back to Broadford and had dinner with Morag from the guest house. They talked until the restaurant closed. She had been a teacher too, it turned out. She had also lost her husband. She asked him if he would come back for the christening, and he said yes before he had even thought about it.

On the morning he left, Dougie walked him to the car.

"Thank you," Dougie said. "For driving me. For everything. I couldn't have done this alone."

"You could have taken the train."

"I could have taken the train," Dougie agreed. "But I wouldn't have knocked on the door."

They stood together for a moment, two old men beside a small pink car, looking at the sea and at the dark mountains on the other side of the water.

"Alan," said Dougie. "Promise me something. Don't let it be another twenty years."

"I promise. I'll phone you every Sunday. At a reasonable hour."

"Eleven o'clock?"

"Eight o'clock," said Alan firmly, and they both laughed.

The baby was born three weeks later, on a stormy night in July. It was a girl, and they called her Jean. Dougie sent Alan a photo of her at four o'clock in the morning. She was very small and very red, and she was wearing a tiny yellow hat.

Alan looked at the photo for a long time in his quiet house in Leeds. Then he went to the bedroom, picked up Helen's picture and smiled at her.

"You were right," he told her. "Life's too short."

Then he went downstairs and started to plan, very carefully, his next trip to Scotland. He printed the maps. He checked the hotel bookings twice. He made a list of petrol stations.

And then, for the first time in his life, he left one whole day of the plan completely empty, just in case.
