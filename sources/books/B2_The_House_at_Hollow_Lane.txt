The House at Hollow Lane

Chapter 1. The Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Clara Bennett would later think was absurd. Tuesdays were for laundry and overdue invoices, not for strangers who changed your life.

It was a thick cream envelope with the name of a solicitor's firm printed in the corner: Hale and Morrow, Market Street, Bridport. Clara opened it standing in the narrow hallway of her flat in south London, still wearing her coat, her keys still hanging from one finger.

"Dear Ms Bennett," it began. "We regret to inform you of the death of Miss Margaret Ashdown of Hollow Lane, Lower Wenbury, Dorset. Under the terms of her will, you are the sole beneficiary of her estate, which includes the property known as Thornfield Cottage and its contents."

Clara read it twice, then sat down on the stairs. Margaret Ashdown. Her mother's younger sister. The aunt nobody talked about. Clara knew exactly three facts about her: she lived somewhere in the West Country, she had "done something" before Clara was born, and her name was not to be mentioned at Christmas. Clara's mother, Helen, had died four years earlier, taking whatever she knew with her.

She had never met Margaret. She had never received a birthday card, a phone call, anything. And now this woman had left her a house.

That evening she phoned Mr Hale. He had a slow, careful voice, the voice of a man who chose every word as if it might be used against him later.

"Your aunt was very clear," he said. "Everything goes to you. She also left a letter, which I am instructed to give you in person, at the house. Not before."

"Why at the house?"

There was a pause. "I'm afraid Miss Ashdown didn't explain her reasons. She rarely did."

Clara worked as a freelance translator. Her life fitted neatly into a laptop and a rucksack, and there was nothing, she told herself, to stop her going down for a week. Nothing except the feeling, low in her stomach, that she was being invited into a story that had started long before she was born, and that she might not like the ending.

She booked the train anyway.

Chapter 2. Hollow Lane

Lower Wenbury turned out to be a village of perhaps two hundred people, a pub called The Plough, a church with a leaning tower, and a shop that also served as the post office. Mr Hale met her at the station in a muddy Land Rover and drove her through lanes so narrow that the hedges brushed both sides of the car.

"Thornfield is at the very end of Hollow Lane," he said. "Beyond it there's nothing but fields and, eventually, the sea."

The cottage appeared suddenly around a bend: low, grey-stoned, with a slate roof that sagged in the middle like an old horse. Roses had climbed the walls and then given up halfway. The windows were small and deep-set, and in the late afternoon light they looked like eyes that had seen too much.

Mr Hale unlocked the door and handed her a heavy iron key and a sealed envelope. "The letter," he said. "I'll leave you to it. If you need anything, my number is on the card. And Ms Bennett?" He hesitated on the doorstep. "People in the village may say things. About your aunt. I'd take them with a pinch of salt."

When the Land Rover had gone, the silence closed around her. Clara stood in the hallway and breathed in the smell of the house: wood smoke, lavender, damp stone, and something else, faint and sweet, like old paper.

She opened the envelope in the kitchen, at a scrubbed pine table with a single chair.

"Dear Clara," the letter said, in small, upright handwriting. "If you are reading this, I have run out of time, and I am sorry. I should have written to you years ago. I tried many times. Every room in this house holds something I could never say aloud. I won't explain it all here, because you would not believe me, and you would be right not to. Look for yourself. Start with the music room. The key is where the robin sleeps. With all the love I was never allowed to give you, Margaret."

Clara read it until the words stopped making sense. All the love I was never allowed to give you. It was a strange thing to write to a niece you had never met. It was the kind of thing you wrote to someone else entirely.

Outside, a robin landed on the windowsill, looked at her with one black eye, and flew away.

Chapter 3. The Locked Room

She explored the house that evening with every light switched on. Downstairs there was the kitchen, a sitting room crowded with books and faded armchairs, and a small study. Upstairs were two bedrooms and a bathroom with a cast-iron bath standing on lion's feet. Everything was clean and orderly, as though Margaret had tidied up for a visitor.

At the end of the upstairs corridor was a door she hadn't noticed at first, painted the same pale green as the wall. It was locked.

The music room, presumably. The key is where the robin sleeps.

Clara searched for an hour. She looked under the doormat, inside vases, behind picture frames. She was beginning to feel ridiculous when she remembered the garden. In the fading light she walked out among the overgrown beds and found, nailed to the old apple tree, a small wooden bird box with a robin painted on the front. Inside, on a bed of dry moss, was a brass key.

Her hands were shaking slightly as she turned it in the lock. The door swung open with a long, unhappy creak.

The room was small and smelt of dust and polish. An upright piano stood against one wall, its lid closed. A window looked out over the fields towards a thin grey line that might have been the sea. And on the other three walls, from floor to ceiling, there were photographs.

Hundreds of them.

Clara stepped closer and felt the floor tilt beneath her. They were photographs of a child. A baby in a pram outside a London terrace. A toddler on a beach. A girl of about seven in a blue school uniform, standing at a school gate, squinting at the sun. A teenager with braces and a bad haircut, laughing with friends outside a cinema. A young woman in a graduation gown.

They were all of her.

Some she recognised, copies of photographs from her own family albums. But most she had never seen before. They had been taken from a distance, from across streets, through fences, from the back of crowds. Someone had followed her through her entire life with a camera.

She sat down heavily on the piano stool. Her heart was hammering. She told herself there must be an innocent explanation, but her mind refused to produce one.

Underneath the window there was a wooden box. Inside were birthday cards, thirty-six of them, one for every birthday she had ever had. They were addressed to her, stamped, and never posted.

Chapter 4. The Man in the Garden

Clara slept badly, in the smaller bedroom, with the door locked and a chair pushed against it. She dreamt of someone standing outside her school gate, holding a camera and crying.

In the morning she woke to the sound of metal scraping on stone. She looked out of the window and saw a man in the garden, digging.

He was somewhere in his fifties, though the weather had made him look older, tall but slightly bent, with a weathered face and a shock of white hair. He worked slowly and steadily, as if he had been doing this for most of his life. Clara pulled on a jumper and went downstairs, her pulse racing.

"Excuse me," she said from the back door. "Can I help you?"

The man straightened up and looked at her for a long moment. Something moved across his face, too quickly for her to read. Surprise, perhaps. Or pain.

"You'll be Clara," he said finally. His voice was deep, with a soft Dorset accent. "I'm Tom. Tom Pryce. I do the garden. Have done for years. Margaret paid me up to the end of the month, so I thought I'd carry on, if you don't mind."

"I don't mind," Clara said, although she wasn't sure. "Did you know her well?"

Tom leaned on his spade. "As well as anyone did, I suppose. Which isn't saying much." He glanced up at the window of the music room and then away again. "She was a private person. Kept herself to herself."

"Did you know she had photographs of me? Hundreds of them?"

He didn't look surprised. That was what Clara noticed most of all, later. He didn't look surprised in the slightest.

"I'm not the one to tell you about that," he said quietly. "It's not my story."

"Then whose story is it?"

Tom looked at her with eyes that were a very particular shade of grey, and for a strange second Clara had the feeling she was looking into a mirror. Then he picked up his spade again.

"Hers," he said. "And yours. Give it time, love. The house will tell you."

He went back to his digging, and Clara stood in the doorway, cold and confused, watching the steady rise and fall of the spade.

Chapter 5. Photographs

Over the next two days Clara went through the house methodically, the way she approached a difficult translation: one sentence at a time, never jumping ahead, never assuming she understood until she did.

In the study she found bank statements, seed catalogues, and letters from a hospital in Dorchester that she didn't read, because she wasn't ready. In the sitting room she found books on birds, on gardens, on music. Margaret had been a piano teacher, it seemed; there were old lists of pupils and receipts for lessons going back to the nineteen-nineties.

And she kept going back to the music room.

She took the photographs down one by one and laid them on the floor in order. Written on the back of each one, in that small upright hand, was a date and a few words. "Clara, aged 3, Clapham Common. She fell over and didn't cry." "First day at school. H. held her hand too tightly." "Aged 15. She has my laugh. I wish I could tell her."

She has my laugh.

Clara sat back on her heels. The thought that had been circling at the edge of her mind for two days now landed, heavily, and refused to leave.

She thought of her mother, Helen: tall, practical, kind in a dutiful way, a woman who never quite seemed comfortable when Clara hugged her. She thought of how she had never looked like either of her parents. Her father, who had left when she was six, had been dark and stocky; Helen had been fair and fine-boned. Clara was neither. People used to joke that she must have been swapped at the hospital. Everyone laughed. Helen never did.

She went downstairs, found her phone, and called the only living relative she had: her mother's cousin, Judith, who lived in Leeds and sent a card every Christmas.

"Judith, it's Clara. I need to ask you something, and I need you to be honest."

There was a long silence on the line. Then Judith sighed, and it was the sigh of someone who had been expecting this call for thirty years.

"You've been to the house," she said. "Oh, darling. I told Helen she should have told you. I told her a hundred times."

"Told me what?"

"I can't do this over the phone. I'm sorry. I'm not the right person. Look in the piano, Clara. Margaret told me once that she kept everything important in the piano."

Chapter 6. The Diary

The piano lid was heavy, and when Clara lifted the top she found, tucked between the strings and the wooden frame, a bundle wrapped in an old silk scarf. Inside were a leather diary with a broken clasp, a hospital bracelet so small it would barely fit around two fingers, and a black-and-white photograph of a young couple sitting on a farm gate.

The girl in the photograph was about seventeen, with wild dark hair and Clara's own crooked smile. The boy beside her was tall and thin, with a shock of hair that was black then but would one day turn white. He was looking at the girl as if she were the only person in the world.

Clara recognised him at once. She had watched him digging in the garden that morning.

The hospital bracelet said: "Baby girl Ashdown. 14.3.1990."

Her own birthday.

She took the diary to the window seat and began to read. The early entries were the ordinary writing of a teenage girl in 1989: school, music exams, arguments with her father, and Tom. Always Tom. Tom Pryce, the son of the man who looked after the Ashdown farm. Tom who could name every bird by its song. Tom who had no money and no prospects and was, according to her father, "not the sort of boy an Ashdown marries".

Then, in the autumn, the tone changed.

"I'm pregnant. I haven't told anyone except T. He wants us to run away to Bristol. He says we'll manage. I believe him. I think I would believe him if he said the sea was made of milk."

"Father knows. I have never seen him so angry. He says I have disgraced the family, that I have thrown my life away, that he will not allow it. He says Helen and Richard have been trying for a baby for years. He says this is a solution. He says it as if I were a problem in an equation."

"They have taken me to Aunt Rose's in Exeter until it's over. T. doesn't know where I am. Father has told him I've gone abroad. I write him letters and Rose promises to post them, but I don't think she does."

Clara read on as the light changed outside. She read about the birth, about holding the baby for one hour before a nurse took her away. She read about Helen arriving at the hospital, pale and nervous, not meeting her sister's eyes. She read about the papers that a seventeen-year-old girl had signed because her father stood over her and told her she had no other choice.

"I called her Clara," the last entry said. "Helen let me choose the name. It was the only thing she could give me, and I think she knew it. I will never forgive Father. I'm not sure I will ever forgive Helen. But I will never stop watching over my daughter, even if she never knows I exist."

Clara closed the diary. The room was dark now. She realised that she had been crying for some time without noticing, the way you sometimes don't notice rain until you're soaked through.

Chapter 7. The Hospital Letters

Clara did not sleep that night. At two in the morning she gave up trying, made a pot of strong tea, and went into the study to face the letters from the hospital in Dorchester that she had been avoiding.

There were eleven of them, covering almost two years. She read them in order, the same way she had read the diary, and gradually the clinical language assembled itself into a picture. A routine scan. Further tests. A diagnosis that the doctors described carefully and without hope. A course of treatment that had been offered, then started, then, after six months, quietly stopped at the patient's own request.

Stapled to the last letter was a note in Margaret's handwriting, apparently a list she had made before an appointment. "1. How long, honestly? 2. Will I be able to manage the stairs? 3. Will I still be able to play?" And underneath, pressed so hard into the paper that the pen had almost torn it: "4. Is it too late to tell her?"

Clara put the note down and pressed her palms against her eyes.

She had been angry all evening. Angry at Helen, who had lied to her every day of her life. Angry at a grandfather she had never met, who had arranged her existence as if he were moving cattle from one field to another. Angry, most of all, at Margaret, who had stood at school gates with a camera instead of walking across the road and saying a single word.

But it was hard to stay angry with a dying woman who had written that question and then underlined it twice.

She tried to imagine the last months in this house. Margaret climbing the stairs more and more slowly. Sitting in the music room with the photographs. Starting letters and tearing them up. Deciding, each morning, that she would phone tomorrow, when she felt stronger, when she knew what to say. And tomorrow becoming next week, and next week becoming never.

If I had known, Clara thought, would I have come? She wanted to believe that she would have. But she remembered herself four years ago, at Helen's funeral, shaking hands with relatives she barely knew, and she remembered noticing a woman standing alone at the back of the crematorium, a woman with dark grey hair and a navy coat, who left before the end. Clara had wondered vaguely who she was and then forgotten all about it.

Now she sat in the cold study with the dawn coming up grey over the fields and understood that she had been ten metres away from her mother, and had let her walk out of the door.

If she had followed her that day, if she had simply asked someone who the woman in the navy coat was, everything might have been different. She would have had four years. Not a lifetime, but four years. Clara knew it was pointless to think like that. She thought it anyway, over and over, until the birds began to sing.

Chapter 8. The Village

The next morning she needed to get out of the house. She walked down Hollow Lane to the village, past fields full of sheep and hedges heavy with blackberries, and went into The Plough for something to eat.

The pub was warm, low-ceilinged and almost empty. Behind the bar was a woman in her sixties with bright red lipstick and an expression of open curiosity.

"You're Margaret's niece," she said before Clara had even sat down. "From London. I'm Pauline. Everyone's been wondering when you'd come in."

"Have they?"

"Small village, love. Not much else to talk about." Pauline poured her a coffee without being asked. "Funny woman, your aunt. Kept to herself. Some people round here never forgave her, you know. After what happened with her dad."

Clara put her cup down carefully. "What happened with her dad?"

Pauline leaned across the bar, lowering her voice although there was nobody to overhear. "Old Edward Ashdown. Fell down the stairs at the farmhouse, back in ninety-one. Broke his neck. Margaret was the only one in the house. There was an inquest. Accidental death, they said. But there was talk. There's always talk. They'd been fighting for years, everybody knew that. Shouting you could hear across the fields."

"Do you think she did it?"

Pauline shrugged, enjoying herself. "I'm not saying she did and I'm not saying she didn't. All I'm saying is, she sold that farm the minute she could and moved into that little cottage, and she never set foot in church again. Make of that what you will."

Clara walked back to Thornfield with her hands deep in her pockets and her mind racing. She thought of the furious, frightened girl in the diary. She thought of a father who had taken her child and handed it to her sister as if it were a piece of furniture. If anyone had a reason to hate Edward Ashdown, it was Margaret.

And yet she found she didn't want to believe it. She had known this woman for four days, only through photographs and handwriting, and already she was defending her, the way you defend family.

The Ashdown farmhouse stood on a low hill two miles outside the village, and Clara found it an hour later by following an old footpath sign and the instructions Pauline had given her, with obvious pleasure, over a second coffee.

It was bigger than she had imagined, a square, solid building of yellow stone, with a new conservatory stuck on one side like an afterthought. A family from Bristol owned it now. Their children's bicycles lay on the gravel, and a trampoline stood where there had probably once been a vegetable garden. Clara stood at the gate and tried to see it as it had been in 1991: the mud, the cattle, a furious old man and a frightened girl at the top of a staircase.

She couldn't. It looked too ordinary. Houses, she was learning, kept their secrets very well.

She was about to turn back when she noticed a car parked at the side of the lane, half hidden by the hedge. A dented green van with garden tools in the back. She knew that van. She had seen it outside Thornfield every morning.

Tom's van.

There was no sign of Tom himself. Clara looked around at the empty fields, and a cold, unpleasant thought began to form in her mind. Tom had a key to Thornfield. Tom had known about the photographs and hadn't been surprised. Tom had been in Margaret's life for forty years and had told Clara almost nothing. And now Tom was here, at the house where a man had died, on the very afternoon she had come to look at it.

She told herself she was being ridiculous. People in villages parked their vans in lanes all the time. He might be working in a garden nearby. He might be walking his dog. But the thought, once it had arrived, refused to leave. She had already discovered that the people in her life were capable of lying to her for decades. Why should a gardener she had met four days ago be any different?

She walked back along the footpath faster than she needed to, glancing over her shoulder more than once. The fields were empty. The hedges rustled in the wind. Once she was sure she heard footsteps behind her, but when she turned round there was only a pheasant, which exploded out of the long grass with a cry that made her heart leap into her throat.

By the time she reached Hollow Lane she was out of breath and angry with herself for being afraid. She was a grown woman who had lived alone in London for fifteen years. She had walked home through worse places than a Dorset footpath in daylight.

And yet, when she turned the corner and saw a tall figure sitting on the bench by her back door, her first instinct was to step back out of sight.

It was Tom. He had a flask of tea beside him and mud on his boots, and he looked up as she approached, as though he had been waiting for her.

Chapter 9. Helen

"I saw your van," Clara said, before he could speak. "Up at the farmhouse."

Tom nodded slowly. "I go up there sometimes," he said. "I walk the old path and stand at the bottom of the hill. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Or penance." He looked at her more closely. "You've found the diary, haven't you?" It was hardly a question.

Clara sat down beside him. For a while neither of them spoke. A blackbird was singing somewhere in the apple tree, and the sky over the fields was the colour of wet slate.

"You're my father," she said at last.

Tom looked down at his hands. They were big, scarred, earth-stained hands, and they were trembling. "Yes," he said. "I am. I'm sorry, love. I'm sorry for all of it."

"Did you know? All this time?"

"Not at first. Edward told me she'd gone to France, to finish her schooling. I believed him for nearly a year. Then she came back, and she told me everything, and by then it was done. The papers were signed. You were in London with Helen and Richard, and we had no money and no rights and no lawyers. And Margaret..." His voice cracked. "Margaret said that if we fought for you, it would tear you apart. That Helen would be a good mother, even if she wasn't a warm one. That the kindest thing we could do was let you be."

"So you just let me go?"

"We never let you go. Not for one day." He nodded towards the house, towards the music room with its walls of photographs. "Every year she'd take the train up to London. She'd find out where you were going to be: your school, your sports day, your graduation. Helen sent her pictures sometimes, in secret. I don't think Richard ever knew. It was the one kindness your mum allowed herself."

Clara thought of Helen, careful and anxious and always slightly apart. She had always believed her mother simply didn't love her very much. Now she wondered whether Helen had spent thirty years waiting to be found out, holding a child who belonged to someone else, terrified of loving her too much in case she was taken away.

"She should have told me," Clara said. "One of them should have told me."

"Yes," said Tom. "They should. We all should. Margaret wanted to, after Helen died. But she was ill by then, and frightened you'd hate her. She kept saying, next month, next month. And then there weren't any more months."

Clara closed her eyes. She felt as if someone had taken the story of her life, torn out the first chapter and replaced it with another, written in a hand she was only beginning to recognise.

"Tom," she said. "The village says she killed her father."

He was silent for so long that she thought he hadn't heard. Then he said, very quietly: "Margaret didn't kill Edward Ashdown."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was there."

Chapter 10. The Night of the Storm

The storm came in that evening from the sea, fast and violent. By nine o'clock the wind was howling around the cottage, rattling the windows in their frames, and rain was being thrown against the glass in handfuls. The electricity went off at half past nine. Clara found candles in the kitchen drawer, lit a fire in the sitting room, and sat with Tom in the flickering orange light while he told her the rest.

"It was March nineteen ninety-one," he said. "Your first birthday. Margaret had found out that Helen was having a party for you in London, and she'd asked her father - begged him - for the address, just so she could send a card. He refused. He said if she ever went near you, he'd make sure Helen moved somewhere she would never find you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstairs a door banged in the wind, and both of them jumped.

"She rang me in tears. I went up to the farmhouse. I shouldn't have, I know. Edward had told me if he ever saw me on his land again he'd call the police. But I couldn't leave her like that. When I got there, they were at the top of the stairs, shouting at each other. He had her by the arm. He was a big man, and he had a temper on him, and he'd been drinking."

Tom stared into the fire.

"I ran up the stairs. I got hold of him, pulled him off her. He turned round and swung at me, and he lost his footing. That's all it was. One second he was there, and the next..." He made a small, helpless gesture with his hand. "I've gone over it ten thousand times. If I hadn't gone up there, he'd still have been alive. If I'd stayed at the bottom of the stairs. If I'd just shouted instead of grabbing him."

"It was an accident," Clara said.

"It was. But who'd have believed me? The farm boy Edward hated, sneaking into his house at night? I'd have gone to prison. So Margaret sent me home. She told the police she was alone, that he'd been drinking and he fell. And she lived with what the village said about her for thirty years, so I wouldn't have to." He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, without embarrassment. "That's who your mother was. That's the kind of person she was."

The wind dropped for a moment, and in the sudden quiet Clara heard, quite distinctly, a single note from the piano upstairs.

They both looked up at the ceiling. Neither of them moved.

"It's the wind," Tom said eventually. "The window in that room has never shut properly."

"Of course," said Clara. "It's the wind."

But later, when he had gone home with a torch through the dripping lanes, she went upstairs with a candle and found the music room window firmly closed, and the piano lid open, though she was almost certain she had closed it.

She did not feel afraid. That was the strange thing. She stood in the doorway for a long time, and what she felt was something closer to being welcomed home.

Chapter 11. Tom

In the morning the storm had blown itself out, leaving the garden full of broken branches and the air washed clean. Clara walked to Tom's cottage on the other side of the village. It was smaller than Thornfield, and neater, with a vegetable patch in the front and a bird table outside the window.

He opened the door looking as if he hadn't slept. "I wondered if you'd come," he said. "Or if you'd be on the first train back to London."

"I thought about it," Clara admitted.

He made tea in a brown pot, and they sat at his kitchen table. On the wall above the sink was a single photograph, framed: a little girl in a blue school uniform, squinting at the sun. The same picture that hung in the music room.

"Margaret gave me copies," he said, following her gaze. "Only a few. I didn't like to have too many. It felt like stealing."

Clara turned her mug round and round in her hands. There were so many questions that she didn't know where to begin, and in the end she asked the smallest one.

"What was she like? Not the story. Her."

Tom smiled for the first time since she had met him, and it changed his whole face.

"Stubborn," he said. "Stubborn as a mule. Funny, when she let herself be. She could make you laugh at a funeral. Terrible cook. Brilliant musician - she could have played in concert halls if things had been different. She was kind to the children she taught, even the ones with no talent at all. Especially those ones. She used to say anybody could teach the gifted ones." He paused. "She talked about you every single day. Did you know you've got her hands? I noticed it the first morning, when you were standing in the doorway. You hold your fingers just the same."

Clara looked down at her hands as if she had never seen them before.

"Will you tell people?" Tom asked quietly. "About Edward. About what really happened."

She thought about Pauline in the pub, enjoying her gossip. She thought about thirty years of silence, and a woman who had chosen to carry the blame so that the man she loved could stay free.

"It's not my secret to tell," she said. "It was hers. And she decided what to do with it a long time ago."

Tom nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders seemed to loosen, as though he had put down a heavy bag that he had been carrying for most of his life.

Chapter 12. What Remains

Clara stayed in Lower Wenbury for another week, then another. She cancelled two jobs and finished a third at Margaret's kitchen table, looking up from her laptop every so often to watch the light move across the fields. Her London flat began to feel like somewhere she had once visited rather than somewhere she lived.

She and Tom fell into a kind of routine. He came in the mornings to work in the garden, and at eleven she brought out two mugs of tea, and they talked. Sometimes about Margaret. Sometimes about birds, or the weather, or nothing at all. It was awkward at first, the way it is with two strangers who are also not strangers, but the awkwardness wore off a little more each day, like the stiffness of new shoes.

She did not take down the photographs in the music room. She thought about it, and decided that they belonged there. But she did add one: a picture Tom took of her on the bench by the back door, laughing at something he had said. On the back, in her own handwriting, she wrote: "Clara, aged 36, Thornfield. She came home."

One evening she sat down at the piano. She had taken lessons as a child - Helen had insisted, which she now understood differently - but she hadn't played in years. Her fingers were clumsy at first. Then, slowly, they began to remember. She played a simple piece, the only one she knew by heart, and halfway through she realised that the sheet music on the stand was the same piece, its pages covered in pencil notes in Margaret's small, upright handwriting.

At the bottom of the last page, Margaret had written: "Her favourite. H. told me. I play it every year on her birthday."

Clara played it to the end. Then she sat in the quiet room, among the hundreds of faces of the girl she had been, and felt that, for the first time, she was not being watched but kept company.

One grey Sunday in October, Tom drove her to the churchyard on the hill above the village. Margaret's grave was at the far end, near the wall, where the grass was long and the view stretched all the way to the sea. The headstone was new and very plain: her name, two dates, and nothing else.

"She didn't want anything written on it," Tom said. "She said words on stones were for people who had nothing left to say. I think she meant the opposite, really. I think she had too much."

Clara knelt down and laid a handful of roses from the garden on the wet grass. They were the last of the year, slightly brown at the edges, but they still smelt of summer.

She had expected to feel grief, and she did. But it was a strange kind of grief, a grief for something she had never had rather than something she had lost. It was like mourning a country you had only seen on a map. She tried to say something, out loud, the way people did in films, and found that nothing came. In the end she just put her hand flat on the cold stone for a moment, the way you might rest your hand on someone's shoulder.

"Helen's buried in London," she said, standing up. "In Streatham. I go twice a year. I used to feel guilty that I didn't go more often."

"And now?"

Clara thought about it. "Now I think I'll go more often," she said, surprising herself. "She did her best, didn't she? With something she never asked for either. Grandfather used all of them. Margaret, Helen, you. He moved everyone around like pieces on a board, and then he fell down the stairs and left them to live with the consequences."

Tom looked out at the sea for a long time. "I used to hate Helen," he said. "For years. Then one Christmas a parcel came for Margaret, with no letter in it, only a photograph of you opening your presents. You must have been eight or nine. Margaret cried for a whole day. After that I couldn't hate her any more. You can't hate somebody who is trying, in their own small way, to be fair."

They walked back down the hill together, slowly, because Tom's knee was bad in wet weather. At the lychgate he stopped and, after a moment's hesitation, offered her his arm. It was a small, old-fashioned gesture, almost shy. Clara took it, and they went down the lane like that, a father and a daughter who were still learning each other's names.

In the spring she sold the flat in London. Mr Hale handled the paperwork with his usual slow care, and when he handed her the final documents he allowed himself a small smile.

"Your aunt would have been pleased," he said.

"My mother," Clara said. "She was my mother."

Mr Hale looked at her over his glasses for a long moment, and then he nodded, as if a file that had been open on his desk for thirty-six years had finally been closed.

That evening, as the sun went down over the fields towards the sea, a robin landed on the windowsill of the music room, looked in with one black eye, and stayed.
